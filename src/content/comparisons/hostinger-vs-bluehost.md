## The short answer

Hostinger is the better buy for most people reading this. It is cheaper on the
48-month term, it renews lower, it bundles a free domain and email, and its
control panel is easier for someone who has never run a website before. Bluehost
still has a real case if you want a phone number to call, a cPanel account that
matches every WordPress tutorial online, and a setup process designed to walk a
beginner through a first site.

The gap is narrower than either company's marketing suggests. Both charge a low
intro rate and a much higher renewal. Both want a multi-year upfront payment for
the headline number. The difference is what you get in the middle years.

## Who Hostinger is for

Hostinger suits people paying their own bill. A portfolio, a service business
landing page, a blog that publishes twice a month, a small store that has not
found its audience yet. Hostinger was founded in 2004, is headquartered in
Kaunas, Lithuania, and says it serves 5 million+ customers across 178 countries,
so it is not a fly-by-night operation — but the product is clearly built around
price and simplicity rather than enterprise support.

The [Premium plan](/plans/premium/) is the entry point: 3 websites, 20 GB SSD,
400,000 inodes, 40 PHP workers, 1 CPU core and 2 GB RAM. That is a comfortable
blog and a thin store.

## Who Bluehost is for

Bluehost suits people who value familiarity over price. It is one of the oldest
names in shared hosting, it runs cPanel, and it has spent years optimising the
first-ten-minutes experience of a WordPress install. If you have read a hosting
tutorial in the last decade, there is a good chance it was written with a
Bluehost dashboard in mind.

It is also the better pick if you want to escalate a problem by voice. That is
not a small thing when a checkout page is down on a Saturday.

## The pricing mechanic, in plain numbers

Hostinger's advertised monthly rate is the total plan price divided by the
number of months. You pay the whole thing upfront. That is the single most
misunderstood part of buying Hostinger, and it applies to Bluehost's discounted
terms as well.

| Plan | Promo rate (48-mo) | Paid upfront | Renewal rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Premium | $2.99/mo | $143.52 | $10.99/mo |
| Unlimited | $3.99/mo | $191.52 | $16.99/mo |
| Cloud Startup | $7.99/mo | $383.52 | $25.99/mo |

The step from Premium to [Unlimited](/plans/unlimited/) costs a dollar a month
and roughly doubles your resources: 2 CPU cores instead of 1, 3 GB RAM instead
of 2, 50 GB NVMe instead of 20 GB SSD, 60 PHP workers instead of 40, and daily
backups instead of weekly. If you can absorb the extra cost, it is the better
long-term buy.

Bluehost's longest discounted term is typically 36 months rather than 48, and
its entry tier generally sits a few dollars per month above Hostinger's at the
same level of commitment. Its renewal also rises well above the intro rate, so
the shape of the deal is similar — the numbers are just less favourable.

One naming detail that trips people up: Hostinger retired the Web Business plan
and now sells it as Unlimited on most pages. Existing Business customers keep
their old limits, so older reviews and forum posts that discuss "Business" are
really describing what is now the Unlimited plan. If you are comparing against a
review from two years ago, check which name it uses.

> Rule of thumb: compare hosts on the renewal rate, not the promo rate. Divide
> the upfront total by the months you actually expect to keep the site, then add
> one renewal year. That figure is the honest price, and it is the one that
> stops people being surprised in year three.

## Performance and infrastructure

Hostinger runs LiteSpeed web servers with the LSCache plugin for WordPress, a
built-in CDN and Cloudflare-protected nameservers. Storage is tiered in a way
that matters: Premium is 20 GB SSD, while Unlimited and Cloud Startup move to
NVMe, which is noticeably faster under database load. Premium's I/O is capped at
12,288 KB/s; the tiers above get 20,480 KB/s. Hostinger also publishes PHP
memory limits per plan — 1,536 MB on Premium, 2,048 MB on Unlimited, 3,072 MB on
Cloud Startup.

Bluehost runs a conventional shared stack. It is fine for a brochure site and
will not embarrass a small blog, but it does not publish comparable CPU and RAM
figures, and it is rarely the host people choose when raw speed is the deciding
factor.

## Control panel and ease of use

This is the clearest trade-off in the whole comparison. Hostinger's hPanel is a
left-sidebar layout — Dashboard, Websites, Domains, Emails, VPS, Marketing,
Files, Databases, Advanced, Account — and a beginner can find the SSL setting
without searching. It is not cPanel, and that is the point.

Bluehost's cPanel is busier, older-looking and far better documented. If you
follow a guide written for cPanel, everything matches. If you have never seen
either panel, hPanel is the gentler introduction.

## Support quality

Hostinger's support is 24/7 in 10+ languages including English, Spanish, French,
Portuguese, Chinese and Arabic, with a median first response on English live
chat of around one minute. Chat-only is the limitation.

Bluehost's support is also 24/7 and adds phone. Reported quality varies more
than Hostinger's, and queues stretch at peak times, but the phone channel is a
genuine differentiator. If you want to talk to a person, this row decides it.

## Email, domains and migration

Hostinger includes 2 mailboxes per site free for the first year on Premium and 5
on Unlimited, plus a free domain for a year with free WHOIS privacy. One caveat
worth knowing before you build: server-based PHP `mail()` sending is capped at 10
emails per minute and 100 per day on a rolling 24-hour basis. For a WooCommerce
store or a contact-form-heavy site, configure SMTP instead of relying on the
default.

Migration is free and automatic. You submit a request, Hostinger's team moves
the site, and it usually takes about 20 minutes with no downtime. Bluehost offers
free migration on most plans too, so this row is close to a wash.

## Ecommerce and scaling

Neither host is an ecommerce specialist, but the difference shows up at the
margin. Hostinger's Premium plan gives you 10 databases with a 3 GB limit each,
40 PHP workers and one CPU core. That carries a small WooCommerce
catalogue at modest traffic. Past roughly 50 orders a month the single core
becomes the bottleneck, and the sensible move is
[Cloud Startup](/plans/cloud-startup/) — 4 CPU cores, 4 GB RAM, 100 GB NVMe and
100 PHP workers for $7.99/mo on the 48-month term.

Bluehost sells ecommerce-oriented plans of its own, and its guided setup makes the
first store easier to launch. What it does not publish is the resource ceiling,
which makes it harder to tell when you have outgrown the plan.

## Which should you pick?

Pick Hostinger if you are price-sensitive, comfortable with chat support, and
want the domain, email and CDN bundled without thinking about them. The Unlimited
plan is the sweet spot for anything beyond a hobby site.

Pick Bluehost if cPanel compatibility, phone support or brand familiarity is
worth a few dollars a month to you. That is a legitimate reason, not a bad one.
