## The short answer

Hostinger wins as a hosting product. It is cheaper per month at the entry tier,
it publishes the CPU, RAM, inode and PHP worker limits you are buying, it runs
LiteSpeed with NVMe storage above the entry tier, and it bundles a domain, SSL,
a CDN and two mailboxes per site for a year. Namecheap wins as a registrar, and
that is not a consolation prize: its domain pricing is consistently among the
cheapest available and it includes free WHOIS privacy for the life of the domain.

The two companies are shaped by their origins. Namecheap grew up selling domains
and added hosting. Hostinger grew up selling hosting and added domains.

## Who Hostinger is for

Hostinger suits anyone who wants the hosting decision finished. A blog, a
portfolio, a small store, a landing page for a service business. Founded in 2004
and headquartered in Kaunas, Lithuania, Hostinger says it serves 5 million+
customers across 178 countries and publishes a 99.9% uptime commitment.

The [Premium plan](/plans/premium/) gives you 3 websites, 20 GB SSD, 400,000
inodes, 40 PHP workers, 1 CPU core, 2 GB RAM and 10 databases for $2.99/mo on the
48-month term. The Unlimited plan at $3.99/mo is the better long-term buy: 2 CPU
cores, 3 GB RAM, 50 GB NVMe, 600,000 inodes, 60 PHP workers, daily backups and a
dedicated IP.

## Who Namecheap is for

Namecheap suits people who think about domains more than they think about
hosting. If you own five or twenty domains, renew them for years, and want a
clean dashboard with free WHOIS privacy and no upsell theatre, Namecheap is
genuinely excellent. It is also a reasonable choice if you refuse to commit four
years upfront — its hosting supports annual and monthly billing.

It is a weaker choice if you want the fastest shared hosting or the deepest
hosting support.

## The pricing mechanic, in plain numbers

Hostinger's advertised monthly rate is the total plan price divided by the number
of months, paid upfront.

| Plan | Promo rate (48-mo) | Paid upfront | Renewal rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Premium | $2.99/mo | $143.52 | $10.99/mo |
| Unlimited | $3.99/mo | $191.52 | $16.99/mo |
| Cloud Startup | $7.99/mo | $383.52 | $25.99/mo |

Namecheap's structure differs in a way that matters. It does not force a
four-year commitment to reach a sensible rate, and its renewal multiple is
gentler than the biggest budget hosts. But its entry hosting rate typically sits
above Hostinger's, and its entry tier is thinner on resources.

> Rule of thumb: price the domain and the hosting separately, even when one
> company bundles them. A "free" domain that renews at the standard rate in year
> two is a one-year discount, not a saving. Compare the renewal cost of both
> line items before you decide.

## Performance architecture

Hostinger runs LiteSpeed web servers with the LSCache plugin for WordPress, a
built-in CDN and Cloudflare-protected nameservers. Storage is tiered: Premium is
20 GB SSD with a 12,288 KB/s I/O cap, while Unlimited and Cloud Startup move to
NVMe at 20,480 KB/s. PHP memory scales from 1,536 MB on Premium to 3,072 MB on
Cloud Startup, and PHP workers from 40 to 100. Data centers cover North America,
South America, Europe, South Africa, Asia and Australia.

Namecheap runs a conventional shared stack. It is fine for low-traffic sites and
the company's managed WordPress product is noticeably better tuned, but the
shared plans do not publish comparable CPU and RAM figures and are not the
reason to choose Namecheap.

One Hostinger caveat to keep in mind: Cloud plans are marketed as unlimited for
websites, but Hostinger's own parameters table caps them at 100.

## Control panel and ease of use

Hostinger's hPanel is a left-sidebar layout — Dashboard, Websites, Domains,
Emails, Files, Databases, Advanced, Account — and it is the easier of the two for
a first-time user. Namecheap uses cPanel on shared plans, which is better
documented and matches the tutorials, wrapped in a registrar dashboard that is
cleaner than most.

If you already know cPanel, Namecheap feels familiar. If you have never hosted
anything, hPanel is the gentler start.

## Support quality

Hostinger's support is 24/7 live chat in 10+ languages with a median first
response of about a minute for English requests. It is fast and adequate for
hosting questions.

Namecheap also runs 24/7 chat and tickets, and its domain support is strong. Its
hosting-specific depth is thinner, and response times stretch at peak. For
straightforward hosting issues both are fine; for a genuinely broken site,
Hostinger's chat is the better bet.

## Where Namecheap genuinely wins

**Domain pricing and renewal.** This is the real win. Namecheap is consistently
one of the cheapest places to register and renew a domain, and it does not inflate
the renewal the way some registrars do.

**Permanent WHOIS privacy.** Hostinger includes free WHOIS privacy too, valued at
$9.99/yr. Namecheap keeps it free for the life of the domain, which is cleaner.

**Billing flexibility.** Monthly and annual hosting billing means you are never
forced into a four-year prepayment to get a reasonable rate.

**A quiet checkout.** Namecheap's purchase flow has far fewer upsell prompts than
either Hostinger's or GoDaddy's.

## Ecommerce and scaling

Hostinger's Premium plan handles a small WooCommerce catalogue: 10 databases at
3 GB each, 40 PHP workers, 400,000 inodes and one CPU core. Once you pass roughly
50 orders a month the single core becomes the limit, and
[Cloud Startup](/plans/cloud-startup/) at $7.99/mo on the 48-month term is the
sensible upgrade — 4 CPU cores, 4 GB RAM, 100 GB NVMe, 300 databases and 100 PHP
workers.

Namecheap's shared plans are inexpensive but thinner on published resources, and
its managed WordPress product is the better-tuned option if you stay with the
company. Neither host raises a warning as you approach a limit, so watch response
times rather than waiting for a 503 to tell you.

The email caveat applies on Hostinger whatever plan you choose: server-based PHP
`mail()` is capped at 10 emails per minute and 100 per day on a rolling 24-hour
basis. Stores and form-heavy sites should route mail through SMTP from day one.

## Domains: when splitting the purchase makes sense

There is a genuinely sensible hybrid here. Register and renew your domains with
Namecheap for its pricing and lifetime WHOIS privacy, and host with Hostinger for
the LiteSpeed stack, published resources and bundled mailboxes.

The catch is that you give up Hostinger's free domain for the first year, which is
worth roughly one domain renewal. If you own one domain, take the bundle. If you
own six, registrar pricing and permanent WHOIS privacy matter more than the
freebie.

## Which should you pick?

Pick Hostinger if hosting quality and total cost are the priority. Go with
[Unlimited](/plans/unlimited/) rather than Premium if you want daily backups, a
dedicated IP and NVMe storage — it is a dollar a month more and roughly doubles
your resources.

Pick Namecheap if you manage a portfolio of domains, want monthly billing, or
simply prefer cPanel. A perfectly reasonable split is hosting with Hostinger and
domains with Namecheap, though you will give up Hostinger's free first year.
